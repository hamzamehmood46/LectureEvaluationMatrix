PRE-REGISTRATION — communication and grammar quality of university lectures
Written 2026-11-14, BEFORE any communication metric was computed and BEFORE any
transcript was read or scored.

WHY THIS EXISTS
A second study asks how clearly and correctly university lecturers speak, using
automatic transcripts of recorded lectures. Automated language scoring is easy
to bend after the fact: thresholds, windows and word lists can all be tuned
until a pleasing pattern appears. This file fixes the measures, the sample, the
analysis and the predictions first, so that the result cannot be reinterpreted.

STATE OF THE DATA AT WRITING
All 103 lectures have been transcribed (faster-whisper large-v3, 2026-11-14).
The only facts computed from them so far are counts: 103 transcripts, 116.0
hours of audio, 1,046,830 words. No transcript has been read for quality, and no
rate, score or comparison has been produced. If that stops being true before
this file is committed, the commit date, not this paragraph, is authoritative.

THE CORPUS
communication/corpus_manifest.csv (103 lectures, each at least 45 minutes).
Three STEM courses: Stanford CS229 Machine Learning (18), MIT 8.04 Quantum
Physics (18), Harvard Stat 110 Probability (18). Three non-STEM courses: Harvard
Justice (13), MIT 14.01 Economics (18), Stanford Human Behavioral Biology (18).
Sampling was evenly spaced within each course by a fixed rule. No lecture is
added, dropped or replaced after this point; if a transcript proves unusable it
is kept in the data, flagged, and reported.

KNOWN CONFOUNDS, STATED IN ADVANCE
C1. Each course has one main lecturer (a few have guests), so course, lecturer
    and field are the same thing. With three STEM and three non-STEM courses, a
    STEM versus non-STEM difference cannot be separated from lecturer style. The
    smallest exact permutation p-value at course level is 1/10 (20 ways to split
    six courses 3 and 3, two-sided), so no confirmatory STEM claim is possible.
    That comparison is descriptive only and will be labelled as such.
C2. Whisper large-v3 tends to drop spoken fillers ("um", "uh") and adds its own
    punctuation and casing. Filler counts are therefore a lower bound, and
    sentence length and grammar rates describe the transcript as Whisper wrote
    it, not the raw speech. Comparisons are valid only within this one system.
C3. No speaker diarization. Student questions and guest voices (notably
    Justice) are inside the transcript and are not removed.
C4. Spoken language breaks written grammar rules legitimately (fragments,
    restarts). Grammar rates are relative indicators, not a verdict that a
    lecturer is "wrong".
C5. A transcript still shows the subject matter, so the judge cannot be fully
    blind to field or institution, even with titles and course names removed.
C6. Lectures differ in length and in how much is board work or silence. All
    rates are normalised (per 1,000 words or per minute of audio).

MEASURES, FIXED IN ADVANCE
Deterministic, computed on the full transcript with word timestamps:
  M1 speaking rate: words per minute of speech (total words divided by the sum
     of segment durations that Whisper's voice-activity filter kept).
  M2 filler rate: per 1,000 words, counting the tokens um, uh, er, ah, hmm and
     the phrases "you know" and "i mean". The word "like" is excluded because
     it is ambiguous, for the same reason ambiguous keywords were excluded in
     the Implementation measure.
  M3 mean sentence length in words, using Whisper's sentence boundaries.
  M4 long-pause rate: gaps of 1.0 second or more between consecutive words, per
     10 minutes of audio.
  M5 grammar rate: LanguageTool (en-US, version recorded) matches per 1,000
     words, using the GRAMMAR category only, as listed in score_comm.py;
     punctuation, spelling and typography categories are excluded.
Model judged, by Qwen2.5 7B Instruct through Ollama, temperature 0, fixed seed,
model tag and digest recorded:
  Each lecture is cut into five non-overlapping windows of 1,000 words starting
  at 10, 30, 50, 70 and 90 percent of the transcript. The judge sees one window
  at a time with no title, course name or institution, and scores 0 to 2 on each
  criterion in rubric_comm_v1.json (clarity of explanation, organisation and
  signposting, definition of terms, use of examples, and correctness of spoken
  grammar). Every score must be backed by a verbatim quote that is checked as a
  substring of the window; a score whose quote does not match is discarded and
  counted. The lecture score is the mean over valid windows.
rubric_comm_v1.json is committed and then frozen before the judge is first run.

HAND CHECK
Twelve lectures, two per course, are chosen with random seed 20261114 from the
sorted manifest ids. For each, the window at 50 percent is read and scored
against the same rubric by hand, with the judge and the deterministic metrics
NOT run and NOT consulted. Hand-scores go in a separate file and are not revised.

PREDICTIONS
P1. Judge validity.
    Spearman correlation between the judge's lecture score and the hand score
    on the 12 hand-checked windows is at least 0.60.
    FAILS IF: it is below 0.40. Between 0.40 and 0.60 is reported as
    inconclusive, not as support.
P2. Judge stability.
    A second run of the judge with a different seed on 20 randomly chosen
    lectures (seed 20261115) agrees within one point on at least 90 percent of
    criterion scores.
    FAILS IF: agreement is below 90 percent, in which case judge results are
    reported as unreliable.
P3. Pipeline sanity.
    At least 95 percent of lectures have a speaking rate (M1) between 110 and
    190 words per minute.
    FAILS IF: more lectures fall outside, which would indicate a transcription
    or timing fault to fix before any other result is read.
P4. Filler undercount (C2).
    The median filler rate (M2) is below 5 per 1,000 words, because Whisper
    drops most fillers. A higher median would contradict C2 and is reported.
P5. Pauses and board work. Stated so it can be wrong.
    The three STEM courses show a higher long-pause rate (M4) than the three
    non-STEM courses, because derivations are written while the lecturer is
    quiet. Descriptive only (C1).
P6. Lecturer dominates.
    Between-course differences explain more than half of the variance in the
    judge score (intraclass correlation above 0.5). If so, any STEM versus
    non-STEM gap is mostly lecturer style, and this is stated in the report.
P7. Agreement between methods.
    The judge's grammar criterion is negatively correlated with the LanguageTool
    grammar rate (M5) across lectures, with Spearman at most -0.30.
    FAILS IF: the correlation is above -0.10, meaning the two grammar measures
    do not track each other.

ANALYSIS, FIXED IN ADVANCE
  Confirmatory: P1, P2, P3, P4, P7, each against its stated threshold, with no
  correction needed because each is a single pre-stated test.
  Descriptive: course means and spreads for M1 to M5 and the judge score, the
  STEM versus non-STEM contrast (P5), and the variance split (P6), with
  bootstrap confidence intervals resampling lectures within course.
  Nothing is dropped for being surprising. Lectures with fewer than three valid
  judged windows are reported and excluded only from the judge-based results.

PROCEDURE, FIXED IN ADVANCE
  1. This file is committed and not edited afterwards. A later change goes in a
     new dated file that says what changed and why.
  2. rubric_comm_v1.json and score_comm.py (all word lists, thresholds and the
     LanguageTool rule set) are committed and frozen.
  3. The 12 hand-scores are written to a separate file and frozen.
  4. Only then are the deterministic metrics and the judge run on all 103.
  5. analyze.py runs once on the frozen outputs. All results are reported,
     whichever way each prediction falls.

RATER
One rater (Hamza Mehmood), who also designed the rubric. This remains the
project's most serious methodological weakness and is not addressed here.
Agreement in P1 is agreement between a person and a model, not inter-rater
reliability between independent experts.

DATA HANDLING
Audio and transcripts are third-party course content. They stay on the local
machine and are git-ignored. Only the manifest, code, rubric, scores and this
file are published.
